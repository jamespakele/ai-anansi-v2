### 3.1 Introduction: The Fog of Words [chapter]
Human attention is finite and shrinking — writers who ignore this lose their readers before the second paragraph.

**Why it matters:** The introduction sets the book's central argument: adapt to how people actually consume content, not how you wish they did. Every rule that follows serves this one premise.

## Problem statement
Readers spend an average 26 seconds on any piece of content; phones are checked 344+ times a day; the brain decides in 17 milliseconds whether to keep reading.
- **26 seconds:** the average time-on-text measured by Ronald Yaros's eye-tracking lab at the University of Maryland
- **17ms:** the window before a reader's brain makes the keep-or-bounce decision

## Root cause 1: equal access to publish
The internet and smartphones gave every person the ability to publish instantly, at scale, for free — creating a volume problem with no natural filter.
- **Result:** more words, more channels, more noise — readers tune out earlier

## Root cause 2: writers still write like it's 1980
Long-form habits persist even as reader behavior changed — word count is still treated as a proxy for depth and importance.
- **The false equation:** longer = more credible, more thoughtful
- **The fix:** repackage, don't dumb down

## Core argument
Adopt Smart Brevity now — write for how people actually read, not how you imagine they should.
- **Not a style preference:** a structural adaptation to the information environment
- **Universal scope:** applies to students, executives, nonprofits, sales teams — anyone communicating one-to-many

### 3.2.1 Short, Not Shallow [chapter]
Smart Brevity is radical repackaging, not simplification — the Axios editorial standard is "short, not shallow."

**Why it matters:** This chapter demolishes the most common objection: that cutting length means cutting quality. It reframes brevity as a skill, not a concession.

## Not dumbing down
Smart Brevity preserves every important fact and nuance; it strips everything that does not serve those facts.
- **The distinction:** summary loses content; Smart Brevity repackages it
- **Violation:** oversimplifying — omitting a caveat or key data point to hit a word count

## Universal scope
The framework applies to anyone communicating one-to-many — students, sales teams, nonprofit directors, C-suite leaders.
- **Not just journalism:** "short, not shallow" is the standard for any field where readers are busy and time is limited

## The core rule
Don't omit important facts — bring caveats back as a tight final bullet if cut too hard.
- **Format:** "**Caveat:** [one tight sentence]" added at the end if nuance is at risk

### 3.2.2 Smart Brevity, Explained [chapter]
The Core 4 structure — tease, lede, axiom, go deeper — is the entire Smart Brevity system.

**Why it matters:** Mastering four elements replaces every other writing framework. Everything in the book is an expansion or application of these four.

## Core 4 structure
Four sequential elements, each doing exactly one job.
- **(1) Muscular tease:** ≤6 words; active verb; the hook
- **(2) Strong first sentence/lede:** one sentence; the single thing the reader must remember
- **(3) "Why it matters" axiom:** bolded signpost + 1–2 sentences of context; adds perspective, does NOT repeat the lede
- **(4) "Go deeper" option:** links or references for those who want more — reader controls depth, not the writer

## Phone rule
Everything should fit on one phone screen regardless of the medium.
- **Why:** most content is consumed on mobile; the phone screen is the real format

## Case studies
The CIA Afghanistan memo and a birthday-party email are the book's before/after worked examples.
- **CIA memo:** analysts buried the key fact in paragraph 3; Core 4 puts it in sentence 1
- **Birthday email:** a 200-word thread reduced to a 6-word subject + 2-bullet body

## Axios HQ
AI-powered implementation platform built on Smart Brevity for enterprise internal communications.
- **Clients:** NFL, NBA, Roku, Austin TX mayor, Edelman
- **Result:** documented 2–3x engagement lift vs. standard internal comms

### 3.2.4 Audience First [chapter]
Picture one specific person — name, face, job title — and write exclusively to them.

**Why it matters:** Audience-first is the first two words of the Axios company manifesto because every other Smart Brevity rule depends on knowing who you're writing for. Without a specific reader, the framework has no anchor.

## Picture ONE person
Before writing one word, name the reader — not "my team" or "our customers."
- **The test:** would this specific person read it if you hadn't written it? If no, kill or rewrite it

## Pope Francis test
Francis cut his homilies from 40 minutes to 10; the nuns applauded most — "they are the victims of our homilies."
- **Lesson:** even the most captive audience has a point at which they check out

## Pastor Glade model
"All you can do is the next right thing" — 9 words that outlasted the entire sermon.
- **Application:** the Big Thought can be stated in one sentence; if it can't, it's not ready
- **Source:** David Glade, Christ the King Church, Alexandria VA

## Lisa Ross / Edelman
"We hide our insecurity in additional words."
- **Reframe:** directness is a competence signal; wordiness signals uncertainty
- **Standard:** if you can say it shorter, shorter is the confident choice

## Tips
Four-step audience-first process before writing anything.
- **Step 1:** focus on one person
- **Step 2:** plot one takeaway
- **Step 3:** write like a human
- **Step 4:** then stop

### 3.3.1 Be Worthy [chapter]
You have 26 seconds — everything after that is usually wasted unless the first words earn attention.

**Why it matters:** Ronald Yaros's eye-tracking data provides the empirical anchor for the whole framework. Writers who don't know this number are setting themselves up to lose readers they'll never know they lost.

## Yaros eye-tracking
26 seconds average "time on text" — University of Maryland research with eye-tracking gear on real readers.
- **Implication:** the opening matters more than anything else you write
- **Attribution:** Ronald Yaros, University of Maryland

## Linda Stone — continuous partial attention
Readers are already thinking about their next notification while reading yours.
- **Not multitasking:** continuous partial attention is a constant drift, not deliberate splitting
- **Counter-strategy:** front-load; don't bury the lead and expect the reader to find it

## Top 4 kick-outs
Four things that make readers abandon content immediately.
- **Too much text:** a wall of paragraphs before the key point
- **Jargon:** words that require insider knowledge to decode
- **Too many choices:** links, options, or questions that require decisions mid-read
- **Long video:** video without a hook in the first 10 seconds

## Megan Green / real estate
"Stick to the facts, be polite — busywork costs money."
- **Real-world application:** bullet-point property updates with color-coded responses; transaction speed increased
- **Lesson:** Smart Brevity is professional courtesy, not a style experiment

## Chris Sacca rule
Write it all first — then make the first 2–3 sentences say it all.
- **Method:** write the full version, then extract the lede from what you wrote
- **Sacca context:** 1.6M Twitter followers; prolific VC writer

### 3.3.2 Grab Me! [chapter]
The tease is the most important words you type — six words to earn the open or the click.

**Why it matters:** Everything else in the framework is irrelevant if the tease fails. The Axios audience team confirmed six words as the optimal mobile subject-line length through A/B testing — this isn't a rule of thumb, it's measured.

## Tease rules
Six hard rules for the headline, subject line, or hook.
- **6 words max:** hard limit; confirmed optimal by Axios audience testing on mobile
- **Active verbs only:** no passive voice
- **Concrete, not clever:** skip irony, jokes, riddles — clarity beats wit
- **One-syllable beats two beats three:** punch over polish
- **Newsy, accurate, urgent:** give the reader a reason to open NOW
- **Never repeat the lede:** tease hooks; lede delivers

## The 6-word rule
Axios's own audience team confirmed 6-word subject lines as the optimal length for mobile engagement.
- **Why it works:** mobile display truncates; six words fit before the cut

## One-syllable rule
Shorter syllables land harder and read faster.
- **Swap examples:** "California COVID strain is more infectious" → not "The coronavirus variant in California is possibly more infectious"

## Eddie Berenbaum / Century 21
Tom Ferry's name in the email subject line drives opens — real-world application of the name-forward tease.
- **Result:** open rates soared for agent recruitment newsletters
- **Lesson:** specificity (a name, a number) outperforms general subject lines

## Before/after examples
Three before/after headline rewrites from the book.
- **"We're shifting plans"** → "New remote work plan"
- **"Following up on our earlier discussion regarding..."** → "Q3 budget: decision needed"
- **"The coronavirus variant in California is possibly more infectious"** → "California COVID strain is more infectious"

### 3.3.3 ONE Big Thing [chapter]
The lede is one sentence — the single thing the reader must remember if they read nothing else.

**Why it matters:** Most writers bury their most important sentence in paragraph three. The lede forces you to commit to the most important thing before you've written anything else — a discipline that sharpens the entire piece.

## The lede definition
One sentence. The takeaway. Distinct from the tease — don't just repeat it.
- **Test:** read only the tease + lede; is the reader informed? If not, the lede is failing

## Elevator test
What would you shout at someone running out the door? That's the opener.
- **Application:** if you can't say it in one breath, you don't know what you're saying yet

## John Bresnahan
"Just tell me something I don't f@$&ing know."
- **What this cuts:** context-setting, background paragraphs, hedging clauses before the news
- **Source:** co-founder Punchbowl News, former Politico

## Cliff Sims technique
Record the story aloud first, then transcribe — the spoken version is almost always already the lede.
- **Why it works:** speaking forces you to commit to the most important thing; writing lets you hide behind structure
- **Source:** Cliff Sims, author *Team of Vipers*

### 3.3.4 Why It Matters [chapter]
The bolded axiom is the frame — one signpost phrase plus one or two sentences that add perspective without repeating the lede.

**Why it matters:** The axiom is the most underused element of the Core 4. Most writers either skip it or accidentally repeat the lede. Done right, it's the moment the reader understands why they should care.

## Axiom structure
Bold a signpost phrase, then write 1–2 sentences of context.
- **Function:** add perspective; answer one of: what changes? what does it signal? what's the larger context?
- **Rule:** NEVER repeat the lede in the axiom — it adds nothing

## Axiom menu
Ten signpost options to choose from.
- **Why it matters** — most flexible; default choice
- **The big picture** — strategic context
- **By the numbers** — when leading with data
- **What's next** — forward-looking
- **The bottom line** — decisional summary
- **Reality check** — corrects a misconception
- **Between the lines** — subtext or implication
- **Catch up quick** — context for late arrivals
- **Zoom in / Zoom out** — level-of-abstraction shift
- **The other side** — counterargument

## Hard rules
Non-negotiables for the axiom.
- **Must be bold:** the visual cue tells the skimmer where the frame lives
- **1–2 sentences max:** not a paragraph; not a clause
- **Adds perspective:** the reader should know something new after the axiom

### 3.3.5 Go Deeper [chapter]
"Go deeper" is the fourth Core 4 element — optional links for readers who want more, controlled entirely by the reader.

**Why it matters:** The go-deeper element shifts power from writer to reader. The writer signals: "I've done the work so you don't have to." The reader decides how far down to go.

## Optional by design
The fourth element is not required; its absence signals the piece is self-contained.
- **When to include:** when source material, data, or extended context exists and would reward a sub-set of readers

## Function
Links and references for those who want the full picture.
- **Format:** "**Go deeper →**" followed by a link or file reference
- **Not a catch-all:** only link what adds meaningful depth, not filler

## Signal sent
"I've done the work so you don't have to."
- **Reader trust:** the writer has already filtered; the link is worth clicking
- **Anti-pattern:** linking for the sake of linking signals the piece wasn't complete on its own

### 3.3.6 The Right Words [chapter]
Every weak word you cut makes the strong words louder.

**Why it matters:** The kill list and keep list are the most practical tools in the book. Memorize them. Most first drafts are 20–30% words that do no work — stripping them is not editing, it's respecting the reader's time.

## Kill list — adverbs
Cut on sight: very, really, quite, somewhat, rather, actually, basically, just, literally.
- **Rule:** if removing the adverb doesn't change the meaning, cut it

## Kill list — foggy qualifiers
Commit or cut: could, may, might, possibly, potentially.
- **Replace with:** planned, considered, discussed, feared, expected, or just the fact
- **Why:** foggy qualifiers signal the writer doesn't believe their own sentence

## Kill list — throat-clearing
Cut immediately: "I know you're busy but..." / "Sorry to bother you..." / "Following up on..." / "I just wanted to..."
- **Rule:** start with the point; apologizing before the first sentence signals low confidence

## Kill list — SAT words and jargon
A sample swap list from the book.
- **avociferous → vocal · prevaricate → lie · conundrum → jam · salient → on-point · ubiquitous → everywhere**
- **Corporate:** leverage → use · bandwidth → time/capacity · synergy → fit/overlap · circle back → talk again · price point → price

## Keep list
Strong words that earn their place.
- **Nouns:** fire, boat, cliff, fish, room — vivid, picturable, one syllable
- **Verbs:** chop, crush, sell, hit, taunt, botch — active, muscular, specific
- **Rule:** if you can say it in one fewer syllable, do it; if you wouldn't say it at a bar, don't write it

### 3.3.7 Emojis [chapter]
One emoji, used well, can replace an entire phrase — but only when the platform and context warrant it.

**Why it matters:** The emoji chapter is the shortest in the book because the rule is short: sparingly and platform-aware. Misuse is worse than no use.

## Use as visual anchor
A single emoji can replace a phrase and break the visual monotony of a text-heavy message.
- **Best use:** subject lines, bullet lead-ins, section breaks
- **Worst use:** mid-sentence decoration that adds no meaning

## Platform context
Emoji rules vary by platform and audience.
- **Do not use ironically:** irony in emoji requires a shared reference the reader may not have
- **One per message** unless the content clearly warrants more (e.g., a visual-heavy social post)

Items ranked by importance; recurring publication name in every issue.

### 3.4.4 Smart Brevity Your Email [chapter]
Subject line ≤6 words. Lede = the one thing. Aim for one screen. That's the entire email standard.

**Why it matters:** Email is where most people spend most of their communication time and where Smart Brevity produces the most immediate, measurable results. The before/after examples in this chapter show 50–70% word-count reductions with zero information lost.

Four non-negotiables for every email.
- **Subject:** ≤6 words; active verb; specific
- **Lede:** one sentence; the thing the reader needs to act on or know
- **Strip throat-clearing:** cut the entire opening if it begins with apology or preamble
- **One screen:** aim for everything visible without scrolling

## Throat-clearing kill list
Five openers to cut immediately, every time.
- "I know you're busy" · "Sorry to bother you" · "Following up on" · "Just wanted to" · "As per my previous"

## Before/after results
Three case studies from the chapter: birthday party, board update, soccer tournament.
- **Reduction:** 50–70% word-count cuts across all three
- **Method:** identify the one thing → lead with it → bullets for anything else → stop

### 3.4.5 Smart Brevity Your Meetings [chapter]
Agenda ≤3 items. Open and close with the one thing. Pre-reads replace long openings.

**Why it matters:** Meetings are the highest-cost, lowest-efficiency communication format in most organizations. Smart Brevity doesn't fix bad culture, but it cuts meeting length and increases decision velocity.

## Agenda rule
Three items maximum on any meeting agenda.
- **Logic:** if you have more than three things to decide, you don't know what the meeting is for
- **Pre-read memo:** distribute a Smart Brevity-formatted memo before the meeting; no time spent on context-setting in the room

## BJ's Wholesale executive technique
Open AND close with "If there's only one thing you remember from this talk…"
- **Effect:** forces the speaker to know the one thing before they start; anchors the audience
- **Source:** BJ's Wholesale executive, cited in ch. 14

## Post-meeting capture
Decisions and action items captured in Smart Brevity format immediately after.
- **Format:** decision (one sentence) + owner + deadline
- **Action item:** verb-first; specific; one person owns it

### 3.4.6 Smart Brevity Your Speeches [chapter]
Big Thought ≤15 words. Open with it. Repeat it. Close with it.

**Why it matters:** Most speeches fail because the speaker treats the story as the destination. The story is the vehicle. The Big Thought is the destination — and the audience should know what it is before you've told a single anecdote.

## Big Thought rule
State the Big Thought in ≤15 words; open with it, weave it through the middle, close with it.
- **15-word limit:** hard cap; forces specificity; if you can't state it in 15 words, you don't know it yet
- **Structure:** Big Thought → story/evidence → Big Thought restated

## Story as vehicle
The anecdote serves the Big Thought — it is not the point.
- **Anti-pattern:** opening with a story the audience doesn't know how to interpret yet
- **Fix:** state the Big Thought first; then tell the story as evidence

## Pastor Glade model
9-word line — "All you can do is the next right thing" — guided the Axios founders for years.
- **Lesson:** the Big Thought can outlast everything else in a talk; make it speakable, repeatable, memorable

### 3.4.7 Smart Brevity Your Presentations [chapter]
≤12 slides. ≤20 words per slide. One idea per slide. Start with the ask.

**Why it matters:** Slide decks are the most common format for burying the lead. Opening with context before the ask wastes every minute of setup — audiences need to know why they're in the room before they can absorb the content.

## Hard limits
Three non-negotiable caps for any presentation.
- **≤12 slides:** if you can't make the case in 12, you haven't made the case
- **≤20 words per slide:** visuals carry the rest
- **One idea per slide:** two ideas on one slide means one idea loses

## Audience-first opening
"Our ask is…" as the first slide — not background, not agenda, not context.
- **Logic:** the audience already knows why they're there; get to the point
- **Anti-pattern:** chronological setup before the thesis; the audience is answering email by slide 3

## Each slide earns its place
Every slide passes a simple test: if removed, does the argument weaken? If no, cut it.
- **Visuals over text:** images, charts, and diagrams communicate faster than bullet paragraphs

### 3.4.8 Smart Brevity Your Social Media [chapter]
The tease is the post. The first sentence is the hook. The link is the go deeper.

**Why it matters:** Social media is the purest test of Smart Brevity — the algorithm and the reader judge you in the first 6 words. There is no warmup, no context, no assumed relationship.

## Structure for social
Core 4 maps directly onto a social post.
- **Tease = the post text:** muscular verb + concrete noun
- **First sentence = the hook:** the thing that makes the link worth clicking
- **Link = go deeper:** the rest lives there; the post must stand without it

## The anti-pattern
"This is a great story and you should read it" — never works.
- **Why it fails:** describes the format, not the content; gives the reader no information and no reason to click

## SCOOP framing
For news and announcements: muscular verb + concrete noun + link.
- **Model:** "City kills downtown bike lane plan. Here's why: [link]"
- **Variation:** name the protagonist ("CEO resigns after data breach"), not the category

### 3.4.9 Smart Brevity Your Visuals [chapter]
Images carry inclusion and communication weight — they are part of the message, not decoration.

**Why it matters:** A Smart Brevity-compliant piece can still fail if the visual contradicts or undermines the text. The visual is subject to the same editorial standards as every other element.

## Images as message
Every image makes a claim — treat it with the same rigor as the headline.
- **Test:** does this image add information? If no, cut or replace it
- **Anti-pattern:** stock "diverse smiling team" photos that have nothing to do with the actual team

## Anti-patterns to avoid
Three categories of visuals that undercut credibility.
- **Trope imagery:** takeout containers for anti-Asian violence stories; crime scene imagery for demographic crime stories
- **Stock diversity theater:** photos of abstract diverse groups in place of actual subjects
- **Decoration charts:** visuals that are pretty but don't compress information

## Charts replace text
Wherever data exists, a chart communicates it faster than a paragraph.
- **Color and font:** signal hierarchy, not decoration; use them intentionally
- **Principle:** if a data point is important enough to include, it's important enough to visualize

### 3.4.11 Communicate Inclusively [chapter]
The inclusive version of a message is the brief version — brevity and accessibility reinforce each other.

**Why it matters:** 65 million Americans have learning disabilities. Plain language, bullet structure, and short sentences don't just serve Smart Brevity — they serve accessibility. The same rewrite that helps a reader with dyslexia also makes the message clearer for everyone.

## The 65M stat
65 million Americans have learning disabilities (1 in 5 children) — plain language and structured bullets directly help this audience.
- **Extension:** also benefits non-native English speakers, the cognitively overloaded — which is everyone at some point

## The brief version is the inclusive version
Cutting complexity doesn't just respect the reader's time — it extends access to the message.
- **Corollary:** a longer, wordier "inclusive" rewrite that softens claims is neither inclusive nor brief

## Swap test
Before including any identity descriptor, ask: would you write "the white executive" in the same place?
- **If no:** the descriptor isn't doing work; cut it
- **Rule:** only include identity information (race, ethnicity, disability) if directly relevant to the point

## Disability references
Only when directly relevant; confirm preferred description with the person when possible.
- **When person unavailable:** ask family, advocate, or representative organization
- **Avoid:** medical-model framing unless the person uses it themselves

### 3.4.12 The Cheat Sheet [chapter]
Four principles, six steps, eleven hard limits — the entire framework on one page.

**Why it matters:** The cheat sheet is the book compressed into a reference card. Return to it every time you write until the rules are automatic.

## Four principles
Authority, Brevity, Humanity, Clarity.
- **Authority:** be a trustworthy source; distill, don't speculate
- **Brevity:** respect the reader's time; short, not shallow
- **Humanity:** write like you speak; real emotion, not broadcast voice
- **Clarity:** frugal with words; every word earns its place

## Six-step process
The complete Smart Brevity workflow in order.
- **Step 1:** define your audience (60 seconds; name a specific person)
- **Step 2:** structure smartly (headline ≤6 words; first sentence = the takeaway)
- **Step 3:** explain significance with an axiom (bold it; add perspective)
- **Step 4:** invent your own axioms (adapt for your industry/voice)
- **Step 5:** bullets for the rest (axioms as section headers; data as bullets)
- **Step 6:** review for accuracy, cohesion, humanity (restore nuance if cut too hard)

## Hard limits
Eleven measurable caps — memorize these.
- **Tease/headline/subject:** 6 words
- **First sentence:** 1 sentence
- **Axiom + explanation:** 1–2 sentences
- **Bullet:** 1–2 sentences
- **Newsletter item:** 200 words
- **Speech Big Thought:** 15 words
- **TED Talk:** 18 minutes
- **Slide text:** 20 words
- **Total slides:** 5–12
- **Meeting agenda items:** 3 max
- **Average attention:** 26 seconds

## The mantra
"Brevity is confidence. Length is fear."
- **Apply it:** every time you reach for an extra sentence, ask what fear is driving it

### 4.32 Smart Brevity Core 4 [note]
The Core 4 is the entire Smart Brevity framework in four sequential elements — everything in the book is an application of this structure.

**Why it matters:** Memorize these four elements in order and you have the complete framework. Every chapter in Parts 2 and 3 is a drill on one of them.

- **(1) Muscular tease:** ≤6 words; active verb; the hook
- **(2) One strong first sentence/lede:** the single thing the reader must remember
- **(3) Bolded "Why it matters" axiom:** signpost + 1–2 sentences of context; adds perspective, does NOT repeat the lede
- **(4) "Go deeper" option:** links for those who want more; reader controls depth
- **Phone rule:** everything fits on one screen regardless of medium
