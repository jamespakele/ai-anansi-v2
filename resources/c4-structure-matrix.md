### 3.1 Executive Comparison Matrix [note]
The 501(c)(4) hybrid model is the only entity architecture that satisfies all three of the ConCon campaign's core requirements: immediate capital reception, donor privacy, and strategic continuity across both the ballot initiative and delegate races.

### 3.2 Ballot Issue Committee — Full Analysis [note]
The Ballot Issue Committee is legally restricted to a single ballot question and cannot support candidate or delegate races — a fatal limitation for the two-phase ConCon campaign.

**Why it matters:** The BIC's 90-day sunset clause and surplus escheatment rule mean the organizing group would be forced to dissolve its financial infrastructure and surrender unspent capital precisely when it needs to pivot to delegate elections.

## Statutory Definition and Operational Exclusivity
The BIC exists under HRS §11-302 and §11-327 for the exclusive purpose of ballot question advocacy.
- **Hard prohibition:** Cannot make expenditures or receive contributions to influence any candidate or delegate nomination or election
- **ConCon implication:** Legally paralyzed after a successful "Yes" vote — cannot fund Phase 2 delegate campaigns under any circumstance

## Formation Triggers and Registration Mechanics
Triggers CSC registration within 10 days of crossing the $1,000 threshold in contributions or expenditures.
- **Emergency window:** If formed within 30 days of an election, the filing deadline compresses to 2 days
- **No DCCA required:** BIC is a CSC-only registration; lightweight to form but rigid in operation
- **Bank requirement:** Depository must be FDIC- or NCUA-insured

## Contribution Limits vs. Absolute Transparency Trade-Off
No contribution limits — a foundation could donate millions in a single transaction — but every donor is fully public.
- **Disclosure threshold:** Name + address required for contributions over $25; employer + occupation required over $100
- **Anonymous contributions prohibited:** Unidentified funds must be turned over to the Hawaii Election Campaign Fund immediately

## The Sunset Clause and Surplus Escheatment Risk
The BIC must terminate within 90 days post-election; all surplus either returned to contributors or donated to a qualifying nonprofit.
- **Escheatment trigger:** Funds not disposed of within 90 days automatically escheated to the Hawaii Election Campaign Fund
- **Operational stress:** Post-campaign accounting delays (media invoices, consultant billing, legal fees) routinely exceed 90 days

## COBRA Initiative Historical Precedent
Bill used a BIC + PAC structure for the COBRA affordable housing ballot initiative approximately 6–8 years ago — but COBRA was a terminal ballot question.
- **Why COBRA ≠ ConCon:** COBRA's goal was achieved or defeated at the ballot box; ConCon requires a secondary delegate election campaign
- **Bill's experience is operationally valuable** for CSC forms and timelines but the structural analogy does not hold for a two-phase campaign

### 3.3 Political Action Committee — Full Analysis [note]
The standard PAC offers cross-phase flexibility — it can support both the ballot initiative and delegate races — but immediately exposing a $200,000 foundational contribution to public disclosure and the "Top 3 Contributors" disclaimer rule makes it unsuitable as the first vehicle.

**Why it matters:** A PAC is the right tool for the campaign window but the wrong container for foundational capital — activating it now trades donor privacy for a modest increase in tactical speed the group doesn't yet need.

## Registration Mechanics and Reporting Rigor
Crosses the $1,000 CSC threshold on receipt of any substantial contribution; triggers an organizational report within 10 days.
- **2026 reporting schedule:** Preliminary Primary Report due July 29; Final Primary due August 28; First Preliminary General due October 1; Second Preliminary General due October 26; Final Election Period due December 3
- **Late contribution reports:** Required within 3 calendar days before an election if contributions over $500 received in the final 14-day window
- **Consequences for non-filing:** Automatic fines; court enforcement; public "Failed to File" listing on the CSC website

## Donor Disclosure and the "Top Contributor" Chilling Effect
Every advertisement from an independent expenditure PAC must carry a "The three top contributors for this advertisement are..." disclaimer listing any contributor giving $10,000+ in the prior 12 months.
- **Effect on $200K donor:** Instant, permanent public identity on every TV spot, mailer, and digital ad statewide
- **Group priority conflict:** Donor privacy was explicitly identified as a key strategic requirement — the PAC cannot deliver it for major early contributors

## Surplus Dynamics and Strategic Continuity
Unlike the BIC, a PAC is not subject to a 90-day termination clock; surplus capital rolls indefinitely into future election cycles.
- **Termination process:** Requires zero balance, settled loans, disposed assets, and formal CSC approval — complex but on the group's own timeline
- **Phase 2 flexibility:** A PAC can pivot seamlessly from ballot initiative advocacy to delegate election support without restructuring

### 3.4 501(c)(4) with Optional PAC Sub-Entity — Full Analysis [note]
The 501(c)(4) hybrid model is the optimal architecture: it can receive the $200,000 foundational grant within 48 hours of DCCA incorporation, remain invisible to the CSC during the capacity-building phase, and activate a connected PAC sub-entity when express political advocacy is required — provided donor consent language is built into the original grant agreement.

**Why it matters:** This is the only sequence that satisfies all four operational requirements simultaneously: immediate fund reception, donor privacy, CSC invisibility during build phase, and legal pathway to electoral advocacy when the campaign window opens.

## Multi-Jurisdictional Formation Requirements and Timeline
The 501(c)(4) requires coordination across three regulatory bodies before it can receive funds.
- **DCCA incorporation (Day 1–2):** File Form DNP-1 with $25 base + $25 expedite fee; creates the legal entity to secure an EIN and open a corporate bank account; this is the "unlock" step
- **IRS Form 8976 (within 60 days):** Electronic notice of intent to operate under 501(c)(4); filed via Pay.gov; $50 fee; $20/day penalty (max $5,000) for late filing
- **IRS Form 1024-A (2–12 months):** Formal determination application; not required to operate but provides institutional donor assurance and tax benefits; operations can commence while pending
- **Hawaii AG Charity Registry:** Required before any public solicitation; filed via Hawaii Charities Online using the Unified Registration Statement (URS); annual fees $0–$600

## Compliance, Spending Rules, and the 50% IRS Threshold
The IRS governs the 501(c)(4) — not the CSC — as long as political expenditures stay below 50% of total activities.
- **CSC invisibility window:** Until the C4 crosses the $1,000 threshold in expenditures made to influence an election or ballot question, it files zero campaign reports
- **Permitted non-electoral spending:** Staff salaries, legal research, issue polling, neutral civic education, digital infrastructure — all CSC-invisible
- **Political cap:** Direct campaign intervention (express advocacy for/against candidates or ballot measures) must remain below 50% of total expenditures to preserve the primary social welfare purpose

## Donor Privacy and the "Consent Language" Compliance Wrinkle
Hawaii's Dark Money transparency laws require written donor consent before any 501(c)(4) contribution can be used for electioneering communications — but executing that consent at the time of the initial donation preserves both privacy and future flexibility.
- **Consent trigger:** If a 501(c)(4) uses contributions for electioneering communications, it must have prior written consent from donors giving $10,000+ individually or in aggregate
- **24-hour disclosure:** Statement of Information required within 24 hours of the disclosure date, listing all donors over $10,000 if electioneering expenditures are made
- **The masterstroke:** Include consent language in the original grant agreement siloing funds for social welfare use *with* explicit consent for potential future PAC transfer — this pre-fulfills the transparency requirement without sacrificing current privacy

### 3.5 Leadership, Governance, and Fiduciary Liability [note]
The structural integrity of the 501(c)(4) depends on a three-member board meeting DCCA requirements and a Campaign Treasurer who is a dedicated compliance professional — not a field organizer.

**Why it matters:** The treasurer role carries extreme personal liability under Hawaii law; misassigning it to Cameron Hurt would expose his personal assets and professional reputation and undermine the entire operation's compliance posture.

## Board of Directors — DCCA Requirements
HRS Chapter 414D mandates a minimum of three board members for a Hawaii nonprofit corporation.
- **Articles of Incorporation:** Director identities are optional in the initial filing but become mandatory in annual reports
- **Registered agent:** Required — must be an individual or registered entity with a physical Hawaii street address; the corporation cannot serve as its own registered agent

## The Campaign Treasurer Liability Nexus
Under HRS §11-324, every noncandidate committee must appoint a treasurer before filing its organizational report with the CSC.
- **Sole financial authority:** Only the treasurer and designated deputy treasurers can receive contributions or incur expenditures; they are typically the only authorized account signatories
- **Record-keeping mandate:** Itemized records for every contribution (name, address, employer, occupation for $100+ contributors); records retained minimum 5 years
- **Personal liability exposure:** Treasurer can be held personally liable for knowing/willful violations, reckless disregard of duties, or deprivation of operative facts — not just fined as an entity
- **CSC enforcement authority:** Administrative fines up to $1,000 per violation; up to 3× the amount of an unlawful contribution or expenditure; false-name contributions are a class C felony

## The Cameron Hurt Vulnerability
Cameron Hurt (Common Cause Hawaii) should lead field operations — not serve as Campaign Treasurer.
- **Incompatibility:** Field organizers operate in rapid-deployment, fluid environments; treasurers must enforce rigid documentation and can unilaterally block undocumented expenditures
- **Risk if combined:** A single audit revealing un-receipted petty cash or misattributed contributions exposes Hurt's personal assets and public reputation
- **Correct assignment:** Appoint an independent, detail-oriented financial professional as treasurer; structurally separate the compliance and organizing functions from Day 1

### 3.6 The "Wall Problem" — Coordination and Information Firewalls [note]
HIPA's 501(c)(3) status is existentially threatened if it coordinates strategy, messaging, or resources with the new 501(c)(4)/PAC political arms — and the violation is presumed the moment shared vendors or personnel appear on both sides.

**Why it matters:** Illegal coordination doesn't require intent — shared vendors, common staff, or synchronized messaging calendars are enough to trigger the presumption, revoke HIPA's tax-exempt status, and implicate the PAC in contribution violations.

## The Mechanics of Illegal Coordination
HIPA is absolutely prohibited from participating in political campaigns under Section 501(c)(3); the PAC becomes an illegal coordinated contributor the moment it acts "in concert or cooperation" with a restricted actor.
- **501(c)(3) prohibition:** Any direct or indirect political campaign participation risks complete revocation of HIPA's tax-exempt status
- **Coordination presumption:** Hawaii campaign law converts independent PAC expenditures into regulated "contributions" if made in concert with, at the request of, or with the suggestion of a candidate, candidate committee, party, or their agents

## Establishing the Safe Harbor Firewall
A formal, written Information Firewall policy is the only safe harbor against the coordination presumption.
- **What must be blocked:** Flow of non-public, material information — media strategy, audience selection, internal polling, fundraising targets, message development — across entity lines
- **Documentation requirement:** The firewall must be a written policy distributed to all employees, consultants, and clients affected; a verbal understanding has zero legal standing
- **Three-pillar architecture:** HIPA (educational) | firewall | 501(c)(4) (issue advocacy) + connected PAC (express advocacy); capital can flow C4 → PAC with proper consent; strategy cannot flow HIPA → PAC under any circumstance

## Shared Vendors and the Personnel Cooling-Off Period
The most easily prosecuted coordination breach is a shared vendor or transitioning employee who carries non-public strategy across the firewall.
- **120-day cooling-off standard:** A vendor or employee serving HIPA cannot serve the PAC for a minimum of 120 days after their HIPA engagement ends
- **Hard personnel decisions required now:** Map staff allocations immediately — who is permanently on the educational side, who is permanently on the political side; transitions are high-risk
- **Vendor exclusivity agreements:** Execute contractual exclusivity clauses with all strategic vendors (media buyers, polling firms, digital ad agencies) from Day 1

### 4.13 IRS Form 8976 [note]
IRS Form 8976 is the Notice of Intent to Operate Under Section 501(c)(4) — a mandatory electronic filing that newly formed 501(c)(4) organizations must submit within 60 days of formation via Pay.gov.

**Why it matters:** Failure to file triggers a $20/day penalty capped at $5,000; the 60-day clock starts at DCCA incorporation, not at first political activity.

- **Fee:** $50 (Pay.gov)
- **Deadline:** Within 60 days of the organization's formation date (DCCA incorporation)
- **Effect:** Satisfies the legal notification requirement to exist as a 501(c)(4); does not grant tax-exempt status (that requires Form 1024-A)
- **Governing authority:** PATH Act; IRS

### 4.14 IRS Form 1024-A [note]
IRS Form 1024-A is the Application for Recognition of Exemption Under Section 501(c)(4) — the formal determination application that, once approved, provides the organization with an official IRS determination letter confirming tax-exempt status.

**Why it matters:** The determination letter is not legally required to operate, but it provides advance assurance to institutional donors, qualifies the organization for nonprofit mailing privileges and state tax exemptions, and streamlines major grant negotiations.

- **Review timeline:** 2–12 months depending on application complexity; operations can begin while pending
- **Key evidence required:** The organization must demonstrate it operates primarily to promote social welfare and civic betterment
- **Filed electronically** via pay.gov

### 4.15 DCCA Form DNP-1 [note]
DCCA Form DNP-1 (Articles of Incorporation) is the Hawaii state filing that creates a domestic nonprofit corporation under HRS Chapter 414D — the foundational step for establishing a 501(c)(4) in Hawaii.

- **Optional at filing:** Director and officer identities (required in subsequent annual reports)
