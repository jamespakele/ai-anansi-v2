# General Smart Brevity rules

Apply to **every** rewrite, regardless of type. Per-type rule files layer on top.

> Source: VandeHei, Allen, Schwartz — *Smart Brevity* (2022)

---

## The four guiding principles

- **Authority** — write as a trustworthy source. Distill, don't speculate.
- **Brevity** — respect the reader's time. Stand out by being short, not shallow.
- **Humanity** — write like you speak. Conversational, with real emotion. Not a Broadway character.
- **Clarity** — frugal with words. Every word earns its place.

> "Brevity is confidence. Length is fear."

---

## The Smart Brevity skeleton (the Core 4)

Every piece of Smart Brevity uses this structure, in this order:

1. **Muscular Tease** — headline, subject line, or opening hook. **6 words or fewer.** Strong, specific verbs. No cleverness, no irony, no SAT words.
2. **Strong first sentence (lede)** — ONE sentence. The single thing the reader should remember. Distinct from the tease — don't just repeat it.
3. **"Why it matters"** — bolded axiom + 1–2 sentences. Tells the reader why this is significant *for them*. Adds perspective; does NOT repeat the lede.
4. **"Go deeper"** — optional. Links / references for those who want more. Says "I've done the work so you don't have to."

Try to fit the whole thing **on one phone screen**, regardless of where it'll be read.

---

## Audience first

Before writing one word:

- Picture **one specific person** — name, face, job title. Not "everyone."
- Identify **what they need to remember** — exactly one takeaway.
- Ask: would they read this if I hadn't written it? If no, kill or rewrite it.

> Test: read the rewrite to a friend. Ask them what the one idea was. If they can't repeat it back, the rewrite isn't done.

---

## Tease / headline / subject line rules

- **6 words maximum.** Hard limit.
- **Active verbs only.** No passive voice.
- **Concrete, not clever.** Skip irony, jokes, riddles.
- **One-syllable words beat two beats three.** Punch over polish.
- **Newsy, accurate, urgent.** Why open this NOW?

**Bad → good:**
- "The coronavirus variant in California is possibly more infectious" → "California COVID strain is more infectious"
- "We're shifting plans" → "New remote work plan"
- "Following up on our earlier discussion regarding..." → "Q3 budget: decision needed"

---

## "Why it matters" — the bolded axiom

Bold a signpost phrase, then write 1–2 sentences of context.

**Common axioms (bold them):**

- **Why it matters** — most flexible
- **The big picture** — strategic context
- **By the numbers** — when leading with data
- **What's next** — forward-looking
- **The bottom line** — decisional summary
- **Reality check** — corrects a misconception
- **Between the lines** — hidden meaning
- **Catch up quick** — context for those joining late
- **Zoom in / Zoom out** — focus level shifts
- **The other side** — counterargument

**Rules:**

- The axiom **must be bold**.
- 1 sentence ideally; 2 maximum.
- **Add perspective**, do not repeat the lede.
- Answer one of: what changes? what does it signal? what's the larger context?

---

## ONE Big Thing

The piece communicates **exactly one** memorable point. If you can't say it in one sentence, the piece isn't ready.

- **One sentence. No exceptions.**
- The elevator-test: if they only see this, is it what you want them to remember?
- After writing, distill: would you yell this at someone running out the door? If not, sharpen.

---

## Bullets

Bullets exist to break dense text and let the eye scan. Use them whenever you have:

- **3 or more related data points / supporting ideas**
- A list, sequence, or checklist
- Anything that would otherwise be a "blob of text"

**Format rules:**

- **1–2 sentences per bullet, max.**
- Verb-first when possible.
- **Bold the lead word/phrase** if there's a key term in the bullet — eye-trap for skimmers.
- No clumps; respect rhythm and white space.

---

## Bold text in body

Bold isn't decoration. Use it deliberately to:

- Mark the **axiom** (Why it matters, etc.)
- Highlight **key names, figures, dates, or terms** the skimmer needs to catch
- Signal where attention should land

Italics are weaker than bold. When in doubt, bold.

---

## Sentence structure

- **Subject. Verb. Object.** Linear, not twisty.
- **Short sentences beat long ones.** If you're gasping for air reading aloud, cut.
- **No nested clauses.** Multiple commas hide meaning.
- One idea per sentence. If you say "and" twice, split it.

---

## Word choice — what to keep

- **Strong, vivid, picturable nouns:** fire, boat, cliff, fish, room.
- **Active, muscular verbs:** chop, crush, sell, hit, taunt, botch.
- **Plain English** anyone could understand at a bar.
- **One-syllable words** when possible. Then two. Avoid three+.

## Word choice — what to kill

**Adverbs:**
- very, really, quite, somewhat, rather, actually, basically, just, literally

**Foggy qualifiers (commit or cut):**
- could, may, might, possibly, potentially → say "planned," "considered," "discussed," "feared," "expected," or just *what is*

**Throat-clearing:**
- "I know you're busy but..." "Sorry to bother you..." "Following up on..." "I just wanted to..."

**SAT words / journalese:**
- avociferous → vocal
- prevaricate → lie
- conundrum → jam
- salient → on-point
- ubiquitous → everywhere
- discourse → talk
- challenge → problem
- posit → assume
- dearth → lack
- disseminate → spread
- elucidate → explain

**Corporate jargon:**
- price point → price
- core competency → skill
- circle back → talk again
- touch base → check in
- bandwidth → time / capacity
- leverage → use
- synergy → fit / overlap

**The rule:** if you can say it in one fewer syllable, do it. If you wouldn't say it at a bar, don't write it.

## Word count + reading time marker

Smart Brevity pieces show their cost upfront. At the top of any rewrite of substantial length, include:

```
*<N> words, <M> minutes*
```

Use **265 words/minute** as the reading-speed assumption.

For pieces under 100 words, you can skip the marker.

---

## What to cut ruthlessly

Before declaring a rewrite done, strip:

1. Every adverb you can lose without changing meaning.
2. Every qualifier ("could," "may," "might"). Commit or cut.
3. Every throat-clearing phrase.
4. Every apology and over-explanation.
5. Every backstory paragraph the reader doesn't need NOW.
6. Every repetition of a point already made.
7. Every word that adds no information.
8. Every fancy synonym used to sound smart.

> If a sentence can be cut without losing meaning, cut it. If a word can be cut without losing meaning, cut it. If a paragraph can be cut without losing meaning, cut it.

---

## The two-second test

A reader decides in milliseconds. The first 6 words and the first sentence are 80% of the work.

- 17 milliseconds: brain decides if you like what you clicked.
- 26 seconds: average time spent on a piece of content.
- 80%: of readers who stop after page 1 of long content.

Plan accordingly. Front-load.

---

## The done checklist

A rewrite is ready when:

- [ ] Tease ≤ 6 words, active verb, concrete.
- [ ] First sentence stands alone as the takeaway.
- [ ] **Why it matters** (or named axiom) is bolded, 1–2 sentences, adds perspective.
- [ ] One Big Thing is identifiable in one sentence.
- [ ] Bullets used wherever 3+ related points exist.
- [ ] Bold marks key names/figures.
- [ ] No adverbs, no foggy qualifiers, no throat-clearing.
- [ ] No SAT words, journalese, or corporate jargon.
- [ ] Word count + reading time marker at top (if length warrants).
- [ ] Reads aloud without gasps.
- [ ] Friend can repeat the One Big Thing back after one read.
