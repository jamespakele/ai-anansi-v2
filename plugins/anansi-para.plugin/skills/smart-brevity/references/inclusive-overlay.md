# Smart Brevity — Inclusive communication overlay

> **Always loaded** alongside any other rule set. This isn't a type — it's a lens.
>
> Source: *Smart Brevity*, Ch. 22

---

## The principle

> If you're not communicating inclusively, you're not communicating effectively.

Smart Brevity is naturally accessible — short sentences, plain words, structured layouts. But it can still fail people when the *content* assumes a default audience.

This file's rules apply on top of every other rewrite.

---

## Audience-accessibility rules

### 1. Write in plain, clear language

- Helps the **65 million Americans** with learning disabilities (1 in 5 children).
- Helps non-native English speakers.
- Helps the cognitively overloaded — i.e., everyone, sometimes.

This is just the general Smart Brevity word-choice rule, harder. No SAT words, no jargon, no insider-isms.

### 2. Bullet points force clarity

When the writer separates ideas into bullets:
- They have to decide what each idea actually IS.
- The reader can scan instead of parse.
- Translation tools handle it better.
- Dyslexic readers track better.

If you're rewriting a paragraph that makes 3 points, the inclusive move and the Smart Brevity move are the same: **bullets.**

### 3. Short, simple, and direct unites people

Complexity confuses. Abstraction alienates. Length loses people. The brevity itself is the inclusion.

## Identity / description rules

### Be specific when writing about someone

- Ask how they identify (e.g., Asian American vs. Chinese American).
- For Indigenous Americans, name the tribe when known.
- Confirm preferred gender pronouns.
- Don't generalize across regions / continents / ethnicities when the specific is available.

### Cut descriptors that reinforce stereotypes

When in doubt, consult:
- [Conscious Style Guide](https://consciousstyleguide.com)
- AAJA, NABJ, NCADJ style guides

### Test by swapping

If you wrote "the Black executive" — would you write "the white executive" in the same place? If not, the descriptor isn't doing work; cut it.

### Omit irrelevant identity information

Race, ethnicity, religion, national origin, disability — only include if **directly relevant** to the point. Otherwise, cut. Naming irrelevant identity perpetuates the idea that the default is white / male / non-disabled / etc.

### Disability references

- **Only when relevant** to the story.
- **Confirm with the person** how they want to be described, when possible.
- If they're not available, ask family, advocate, or representative organization.
- Avoid medical-model framing unless the person uses it themselves.

## Visual / image rules

### Don't reinforce tropes

- Illustrating an article on anti-Asian violence with takeout containers.
- Using stock photos of "diverse smiling team" that have nothing to do with the actual team.
- Pairing crime stories with images of people who match a profile but aren't the people in the story.

The image is part of the message. It carries inclusion weight too.

---

## What this overlay changes about a rewrite

When applied on top of email / newsletter / memo / etc., the inclusive overlay adds these checks:

- [ ] Every identity descriptor is **necessary and specific**. Cut the rest.
- [ ] Every example covers more than one default audience (no "imagine you're a CEO with a wife and kids" framing in a general-audience piece).
- [ ] Every metaphor / sports reference / cultural touchstone works for non-default readers.
- [ ] Every visual passes the trope check.
- [ ] Reading-level stays accessible — short sentences, plain words, bulleted structure.

If any check fails, revise.

---

## What this overlay does NOT do

- Does not add tokenism or performative inclusion language.
- Does not soften specific claims into vague ones.
- Does not sacrifice brevity for "inclusive" verbosity.
- Does not require re-stating identity in every reference.

The goal: **the inclusive version is the brief version**. They reinforce each other. If your "more inclusive" rewrite is longer and softer, you've gone wrong.
