# Words are killing your ideas. Here's the fix.

*~2,400 words, 9 minutes*

The Smart Brevity system — tease, lede, axiom, go deeper — cuts through the noise and gets you heard in a world where readers give you 26 seconds.

**Why it matters:** The average person checks their phone 344 times a day. Your emails go unread. Your meetings lose the room. Your slides put people to sleep. Smart Brevity is the antidote — and it's teachable in an afternoon.

---

## Introduction: The Fog of Words

You're drowning in words — and so is everyone you're trying to reach.

**Why it matters:** Eye-tracking studies give you 26 seconds of average attention per piece of content. One-third of work emails go unread. The problem isn't them — it's how you write.

- **344 times a day:** average phone checks — once every 4 minutes.
- **17 milliseconds:** how long the brain needs to decide if it likes what it clicked.
- **Two root causes:** technology opened the floodgates; bad habits keep them open.

## Ch 1: Short, Not Shallow

"Brevity is confidence. Length is fear."

**Why it matters:** Smart Brevity isn't dumbing down — it's stripping away everything that doesn't serve the reader. Short, not shallow. Every time.

- Say **MORE** in **LESS** time — give readers what's new and why it matters, then stop.
- The biggest beneficiaries are your readers, not you.

## Ch 2: The Core 4, Explained

Smart Brevity is a four-part structure: tease, lede, axiom, go deeper.

**Why it matters:** Most people bury the point in a fog of words. The Core 4 is a straitjacket on your worst communication instincts — replicable, teachable, and fast.

- **Muscular tease:** ≤6 words. Active verb. Concrete. No irony.
- **Strong lede:** One sentence. The ONE thing they must remember.
- **Axiom ("Why it matters"):** 1–2 sentences of context. Bolded. Adds perspective — never repeats the lede.
- **Go deeper:** Optional. Links for those who want more.

**The bottom line:** If you see everything, you remember nothing.

## Ch 4: Audience First

Picture one specific person before you type a single word.

**Why it matters:** Most communication fails because writers think about what THEY want to say — not what the reader needs to hear. Flip that and everything changes.

- **The test:** ask a friend to repeat your One Big Thing back after one read. If they can't, rewrite it.
- **The hack:** talk about your point out loud first. Then write it down. That's your lede.
- **The close:** just say it. Then stop. Cowards hide in clauses.

## Ch 5: Be Worthy

People give you 26 seconds. Earn them.

**Why it matters:** Professor Ronald Yaros's eye-tracking research shows readers are in "continuous partial attention" — even when they're looking at your words, they're thinking about the next alert. Only essential content survives.

- **Top 4 reader "kick-outs":** too much text, too much jargon, too many choices, long video.
- **The rule:** list your points in order of importance — the first is the one most likely to stick. You pick; don't make them pick.

## Ch 6: Six Words to Rule the Inbox

The tease is 80% of the work.

**Why it matters:** The subject line, headline, or opening hook is the decision point. Lose them there and nothing else matters — not the brilliant insight buried in paragraph four.

- **Hard limit:** 6 words, tops. Active verb. No irony, cleverness, or SAT words.
- **One-syllable words beat two; two beat three.** Punch over polish.
- **The gut-check:** would you read it if you hadn't written it?

*Before → After:*
- "The coronavirus variant in California is possibly more infectious" → **"California COVID strain is more infectious"**
- "Product sprint recap for us to review" → **"Sprint recap: 7 new templates"**

## Ch 7: ONE Big Thing

One sentence. One point. No exceptions.

**Why it matters:** Your reader is scanning, not reading word-for-word. They need the point handed to them. The lede is your one shot — and most writers blow it by warming up instead of leading.

- **The elevator test:** if they're running out the door, what's the ONE thing you'd shout?
- **Mike's rule:** after every interview or event, call someone and say what happened. That's your first sentence. Every time.
- **What to skip:** anecdotes, jokes, showing off, warming up. Start with the point.

---

## Ch 8: The Bolded Axiom

"Why it matters" is a street sign. Use it.

**Why it matters:** Readers don't just need to know WHAT happened — they need to know why it matters to THEM. The axiom hands them that context instantly without making them work for it.

- **Bold it. Always.** "Why it matters" is the most universal; invent your own for your brand or voice.
- **1–2 sentences max.** Adds perspective; never repeats the lede.
- Other axioms: **The big picture · The bottom line · By the numbers · What's next · Reality check · Between the lines**

---

## Ch 9: Go Deeper

Give readers the rabbit hole — then let them choose.

**Why it matters:** "Go deeper" signals thoroughness without burying anyone in words. Most won't click. They don't need to — just seeing it builds trust. It says: *I've done the work so you don't have to.*

- **Bullets** for 3+ related points. The eye scans bullets; it drowns in blobs.
- **Bold key terms** — the skimmer's eye-trap.
- **Stop.** The biggest flaw in all communication is overwriting. Just stop.

---

## Ch 10: The Right Words

Short, vivid, picturable words win every time.

**Why it matters:** The gap between the almost-right word and the right word is the gap between lightning and a lightning bug. Smart writing is linear — Subject. Verb. Object. — and anyone at a bar could understand it.

- **Keep:** fire, boat, cliff / chop, taunt, botch, crush — one-syllable nouns and verbs rule.
- **Kill:** very, really, potentially, leverage, synergy, bandwidth, circle back, price point.
- **The rule:** if you wouldn't say it at a bar, don't write it.

---

## Ch 11: Emojis

One emoji can replace twenty words. 🔥

**Why it matters:** Used sparingly, emojis signal tone instantly — saving time and putting readers in the right headspace before a single word is processed. An emoji in a subject line makes your email pop in a crowded inbox.

- **Business-ready:** 🚨 (breaking), 📊 (data), 🔥 (important), ⏰ (deadline), 💡 (idea)
- **The rule:** restraint. One at the right moment beats five scattered anywhere.

## Ch 13: The Art of the Newsletter

A smart newsletter gets read. A long one doesn't.

**Why it matters:** Newsletters work because they bring order to chaos — and they create FOMO. Nobody wants to be the one who missed the wedding announcement tucked at the end.

- **Name:** 1–2 words, punchy and clear.
- **Length:** under 1,000 words total; 200 words per item, tops.
- **Items:** 5–10, numbered, in order of importance. Open with "1 big thing: [headline]."
- **Reading time:** tell them upfront. Respect their clock.
- **End** with something funny or personal — a smile to go.

Smart Brevity is your biggest professional edge.

**Why it matters:** 70% of employees want shorter communications at work (Gallup). Remote and hybrid work made clear, crisp communication the central competitive skill — and most people are terrible at it.

## Ch 15: Smart Brevity Your Email

70% of employees want shorter emails. Give it to them.

**Why it matters:** Inboxes now receive 126 emails/day — up from 50 in 2005. A bad subject line means your email never gets read. A bloated body means it gets skimmed or deleted.

- **Subject line:** short, direct, urgent — tells me why I MUST open it NOW.
- **First sentence:** always the ask or the news. Never the warm-up.
- **Bullets:** the skimmer's lifeline — use them for all supporting points.
- **Bold** the key names, figures, and dates you need them to catch.

## Ch 16: Smart Brevity Your Meetings

Most meetings are too long. Fix yours before it starts.

**Why it matters:** 90% of people daydream in meetings. 72% do other work during them (HBR). The pregame — a one-sentence objective and 3-bullet agenda sent the night before — determines whether your meeting is useful before anyone shows up.

- **Before:** 1-sentence objective + 3-bullet agenda, the night before.
- **During:** start on time, state the objective, hold to 20 minutes.
- **After:** email decisions and action items while it's fresh — it often prevents the next meeting.

## Ch 17: Smart Brevity Your Speeches

Nobody ever wished a speech went longer.

**Why it matters:** The audience will remember one point. One. Your job is to choose it, sharpen it into ≤15 words, state it at the top, support it, and repeat it at the end. Everything else is decoration.

- **Open** with a real story — neighbor-length. Set up, situation, happened, stop.
- **State the Big Thought up front:** "The one thing I want you to remember today is..."
- **TED rule:** 18 minutes max — "short enough to hold attention, long enough to matter."
- **Always end graciously.** It makes people want to cheer.

## Ch 18: Smart Brevity Your Presentations

Simplify to exaggerate. Fewer slides, less text, more recall.

**Why it matters:** Text is the least effective way to communicate in a presentation. Images + few words = 65% recall vs. 10% for words alone (molecular biologist John Medina). Minimalism is the goal.

- **One message per slide.** Readable in 3 seconds — think billboard at 65 mph.
- **≤20 words per slide.** If more, try again.
- **McKinsey rule:** for every 20 slides you want, use 2.
- **Last slide:** your specific ask. "I created this deck so I can get ___."

## Ch 19: Smart Brevity Your Social Media

Social media is hand-to-hand attention combat.

**Why it matters:** On a social feed you don't have seconds — you have a blink. Smart Brevity's clean structure gives your posts the punch to stop the scroll. Give something; don't just ask for a click.

- **Twitter:** facts, data, hot quotes, breaking news — the more urgent, the better.
- **Instagram:** arresting image + slim, muscular text.
- **Facebook:** provocative framing on a solid idea — snore and it vanishes.
- **Everywhere:** one thing, sharply said. Tease them with the nugget; let the story do the rest.

## Ch 20: Smart Brevity Your Visuals

Good hierarchy goes unnoticed. Bad hierarchy draws all the wrong attention.

**Why it matters:** Visuals carry the same audience-first obligation as words. An image that confuses the eye or reinforces a trope undermines everything the writing worked for.

- **Start with a strong concept.**
- **Edit out superfluous elements** — negative space is your friend.
- **Judge from the viewer's eyes,** not the designer's.

## Ch 22: Communicate Inclusively

Brevity and inclusion reinforce each other. Always.

**Why it matters:** 65 million Americans have learning disabilities. Millions more read English as a second language. Smart Brevity's plain, structured style makes it naturally accessible — that's a feature, not a side effect.

- **Be specific:** ask how people identify; name the tribe when known; confirm pronouns.
- **Swap test:** substitute one identity for another. If the language shifts, revise it.
- **Omit irrelevant descriptors.** If you'd only note it for one group, cut it entirely.

## Ch 23: The Cheat Sheet

Four principles. One checklist. Use it every time.

**The four principles:**
